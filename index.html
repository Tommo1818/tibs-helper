Week 2 • Theme 1 (With Theme 0 Preamble)

Macro Paradigms (Carlota Perez) vs. Firm-Level Disruption (Clayton Christensen)

Core Comparison (10 Marks in Exam): Perez and Christensen both examine technological displacement, but at contrasting scales. Perez models 50–60 year macroeconomic techno-economic paradigms driven by financial speculation bubbles and socio-institutional restructuring. Christensen models microeconomic firm-level competition where rational incumbents overshoot customer needs and lose to low-end or new-market entrants.

2. Clayton Christensen's Disruptive Innovation Trajectories

Click the trajectory buttons below to isolate incumbent overshooting and entrant disruption.

Christensen's Core Rubric Points (10 Marks)

1. Sustaining vs. Disruptive (3m): Sustaining improves performance along dimensions valued by mainstream customers. Disruptive starts inferior on traditional metrics, cheaper/simpler, establishing a low-end foothold (overserved users) or new-market foothold (non-consumers).

2. Why Rational Incumbents Fail (2m): Resource allocation rationally chases high-margin products for existing premium customers, leaving the low end undefended.

3. Upward Trajectory (2m): Entrants iterate until their quality satisfies mainstream needs, taking market volume from below.

4. Contrast with Perez (3m): Christensen focuses on firm-level micro competition; Perez focuses on macroeconomic 50-year surges and financial capital bubbles.

3. Level 7 Academic Critiques of Disruption Theory

Julian Birkinshaw — Incumbent Resilience & 4 Strategies

Disruption is rarely rapid, incumbents are not blind, and incumbent failure is not inevitable. Established firms hold moats (scale, brand, regulatory licences) and fight back via: (1) Disrupt the disruptor, (2) Co-opt/acquire them, (3) Retreat to high-margin niches, or (4) Diversify.

Jill Lepore & Michael Weeks — Methodological Flaws

Lepore (2014): Highlights hand-picked survivorship bias in Christensen’s cases (many cited disruptors later collapsed while incumbents adapted). Weeks (2015): Notes disruption theory is circular and fails to distinguish between technological vs. business-model innovation.

Haydn Shaughnessy — Ecosystems & The iPhone Anomaly

Christensen famously predicted the Apple iPhone would fail because it was an expensive, high-end sustaining device against Nokia. He missed how platform ecosystems (App Store) create value across the top and bottom of the market simultaneously.

David Edgerton (Shock of the Old) & Joseph Schumpeter

Edgerton: Critiques "innovation-centric" bias; society relies far more on humble technologies-in-use and maintenance (corrugated iron, sewing machines) than frontier novelties. Schumpeter: Coined Creative Destruction — capitalism incessantly revolutionises from within.

Week 4 • Theme 3

Organising R&D (PARC vs. Open Innovation) & Financing (Mazzucato vs. Westlake)

Two Core Enquiries: (1) How should corporations organise R&D? (Centralised research labs vs. Open Innovation vs. Hybrid models). (2) Who takes the true risk in financing breakthrough technologies? (The Entrepreneurial State vs. private venture capital).

1. Organising Innovation: Centralised Research Labs vs. Open Innovation

Centralised Labs
Bell Labs & Xerox PARC

Strengths: Long-term horizon, deep interdisciplinary research insulated from quarterly revenue (transistor, laser, UNIX, GUI, Ethernet, laser printer).

Why Xerox Failed to Monetise GUI (The PARC Dilemma): (1) Core rigidity: Xerox saw itself as a copier company; (2) Architectural filtering (Henderson & Clark); (3) Weak appropriability & missing complementary assets (Teece, 1986).

Open Innovation
Henry Chesbrough (2003)

Core Premise: "Not all the smart people work for us." Knowledge flows across permeable organizational boundaries.

Inbound: Licensing external IP, startup acquisitions, academic partnerships.
Outbound: Spinning out non-core tech or open-sourcing software (Google open-sourcing TensorFlow/Kubernetes) to shape ecosystem standards.

Hybrid / Ambidexterity
What Stays In vs. Out?

Seminar Slide 6 Rule: Never just recommend "hybrid" — specify what is internal and what is external!

Level 7 Model: Keep core safety-critical IP, proprietary data architectures, and long-term moonshots internal (via O'Reilly & Tushman structural ambidexterity), while using external open innovation for speed to market and peripheral tooling.

2. Financing Innovation: Valley of Death & Mazzucato vs. Westlake

The Debate: Whose Risk? Whose Reward?

Mariana Mazzucato (The Entrepreneurial State): Foundational technologies in the iPhone (Internet, GPS, touchscreen, Siri, microchips) were funded at TRL 1–4 by public agencies (DARPA, NSF, CIA). Critiques "socialising risks while privatising rewards" and argues the state should retain equity/royalties to fund future waves.

Stian Westlake's Critique (Capitalism without Capital / Nesta):
1. Tech-centric bias: Mazzucato equates innovation solely with lab R&D, ignoring massive private investments in software design, supply chains, and business models.
2. State survivorship bias: Celebrates the Internet while ignoring costly state commercial flops (Concorde, Minitel, breeder reactors).
3. Complementary risks: Early scientific research does not remove massive downstream commercial scaling risks.

3. Startup Execution: Lean Startup (Ries) vs. Blitzscaling (Hoffman)

Guy Kawasaki — Art of the Start

Make Meaning: Create a 3-word mantra, "Jump to the Next Curve" (ice harvesting → ice factory → refrigerator), and craft DICEE products (Deep, Intelligent, Complete, Empowering, Elegant).

Eric Ries — The Lean Startup

Validated Learning: Treat startups as hypotheses under uncertainty. Rapid Build–Measure–Learn MVP feedback loops to decide whether to Pivot or Persevere. Limitation: Risks getting stuck in incremental local maxima.

Reid Hoffman — Blitzscaling

Speed Over Efficiency: Burn capital aggressively in winner-take-all markets to capture global network scale first. Kuratko et al. Critique: Without true network moats, blitzscaling leads to catastrophic capital destruction and governance fraud (WeWork, Theranos).

2. Open Source Dynamics: Eric Raymond (1999) vs. Nadia Eghbal (2020)

Eric Raymond — Cathedral & Bazaar

Contrasts top-down Cathedral development with decentralized Bazaar collaboration (Linux). Famous rule — Linus's Law: "Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow." Assumes peer contribution scales effortlessly.

Nadia Eghbal — Working in Public

Empirical rebuttal: modern open source is rarely a communal bazaar. Most critical repos are Stadiums (1 overworked maintainer + thousands of passive free-riding users). Modern tech rests on unpaid digital infrastructure (Heartbleed, Log4j, XZ Utils backdoor).

Permissive vs. Copyleft Licences

Permissive (MIT, Apache 2.0): Can be modified and embedded into closed-source commercial software with minimal restrictions.
Copyleft / Viral (GPLv2, GPLv3): Any distributed derivative work must also be released as open source under GPL terms.

Meaningful Human Review Diagnostics (Slide 9 & 13 Cases)

GDPR Art. 5 Core Principles: (1) Lawfulness, fairness, transparency; (2) Purpose limitation; (3) Data minimisation; (4) Accuracy; (5) Storage limitation; (6) Integrity & confidentiality; (7) Accountability.

Why "Rubber-Stamping" Fails (Slide 9 Retailer Case): A staff member clicking "approve" without examining evidence does not make an automated account closure human — it remains a solely automated decision requiring statutory safeguards.

Why "Data-Error-Only Review" Fails (Slide 13 Admissions Case): Offering human review only when an applicant identifies an input data error violates UK DUAA 2025 safeguards! Applicants cannot see proprietary model weights, and the right to contest covers unfair algorithmic inferences and edge cases, not just typos.

How to Test Human Review: Audit review duration and evidence-access logs, track override/reversal rates, and inject blind "seeded error" test cases into the review queue.
